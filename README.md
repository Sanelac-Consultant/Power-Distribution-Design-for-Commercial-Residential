## Load Behaviour Changes Under Every Roof

An office behaves like a steady, daytime animal. Lighting, UPS-backed IT rooms, and above all air conditioning stack up between ten and six, then fall away. A housing society is the opposite: a quiet afternoon, then a sharp evening spike as air conditioners, induction cooktops, geysers and pumps all wake together.

Designing both with the same demand assumptions is the most common early mistake. Transformers and incomers are sized on maximum demand, not connected load, and diversity factors must reflect real occupancy habits. Too tight and the building is starved by its second summer; too generous and the client pays for idle copper.

## Substation Placement and Supply Voltage

Once demand is credible, the next call is supply level and where the transformers live. Large campuses and townships usually take HT supply and step down internally, while smaller blocks stay on LT connections. The tempting choice is to place the substation wherever the plot has spare space.

The smarter choice is the load centre, because every extra metre of heavy LT cable adds cost, voltage drop and heat for the life of the building. In a tall tower, zoned supply often beats one enormous riser, since faults stay local. ## Risers, Busbars and the Cable Question

In high-rise commercial work, busbar trunking has become the default riser: compact, easy to tap on every floor and lower in losses than long cable runs. Cables still win for small or changing loads.

Residential towers suit a mixed approach, with busbar on the main spine and cable for the final run to each flat. Whatever the choice, check voltage drop at the farthest point under full load.

## Protection Studies Nobody Photographs

Ribbon-cutting photos never feature a protection coordination study, yet it decides whether a fault takes out one circuit or an entire building. Selectivity, correct fault ratings and earthing that survives Delhi’s dry summers are the unglamorous details. Before freezing the single-line diagram, three checks are worth the time:

- Fault level at every board against the breaking capacity of the installed device
- Discrimination between incomer, sub-main and final circuit settings
- Earth pit resistance planning for dry soil, with maintenance access

Residential blocks should add RCCB and surge protection at every flat’s board, which costs little next to a burnt-out compressor.

## Residential Distribution Needs Its Own Playbook

Homes are not small offices. Each flat needs a zoned distribution board with separate circuits for air conditioners, kitchen appliances and lighting, while the society needs independent feeders for lifts, pumps, fire systems and common areas. Two newer loads are rewriting the brief.

EV charging is arriving in basements faster than sanctioned-load calculations expected, and rooftop solar, boosted by the PM Surya Ghar household subsidy, needs a clear path for net metering and safe isolation. Spare ways and spare rising-main capacity cost a fraction of re-cabling later.

## Power Quality in the 2026 Building

LED drivers, variable frequency drives and EV chargers all inject harmonics that overheat neutrals and shorten transformer life. Oversized neutrals, harmonic-duty transformers and power quality meters at the main incomer are modest additions at design stage.

Sub-metering by floor or tenant adds something owners now prize: data that lets facility teams spot a drifting load before a breaker trips.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1. Does a residential society need HT supply?

Not always. Societies whose contract demand crosses the utility’s LT limit, usually a few hundred kVA, take HT supply and install their own transformers. Smaller blocks stay on LT. The exact threshold depends on the distribution licensee, so confirm it early.

### Q2. Is 20% spare capacity enough for a new commercial building?

Yes, between 20 and 30 percent is a practical range for most offices. Anything lower leaves no room for tenant fit-outs, extra cooling, or future EV chargers. Buildings with data rooms or planned expansion should lean toward the higher end.

### Q3. Should EV chargers sit on a dedicated feeder?

Yes, a dedicated feeder is the safer approach. Chargers draw long, continuous loads that can overheat shared circuits. A separate feeder with its own protection and metering also lets load management software cap total demand, protecting lifts, pumps and lighting during evening peaks.

### Q4. Is busbar trunking better than cables for high-rise risers?

For commercial towers, usually yes. Busbar trunking saves shaft space, lowers losses, and allows quick floor-level tap-offs. Cables stay competitive for smaller loads or irregular layouts, and a hybrid riser with busbar on the main spine suits many residential towers.
